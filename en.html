Industry Background

Good Banking Corporation (GBC) is a full-service bank that has run its "Future Financial Leaders Program" for five years. It has both a corporate banking division that serves business clients and a retail banking division that serves individual customers. The program selects top performers from both divisions for a six-month course designed to build leadership and strategic thinking skills, and the company has consistently promoted it as a flagship talent-development success story — participants' competency scores climb every year, and satisfaction surveys have always come back high.

The problem is that this "success" isn't backed up by the numbers. During a routine quarterly data review, Lee In-sa on the HRD team found something troubling: branches with more program graduates should logically show higher customer satisfaction and profitability, but in practice there's almost no such trend. Individual competency scores are clearly rising, yet that growth isn't translating into real performance at the branch level. Lee In-sa also discovers that this isn't the first time the issue has come up — a nearly identical concern was raised internally three years ago, but it was never followed up on.

Core Dilemma — Leadership is asking whether the company should keep investing in this program, and Lee In-sa, who handles talent-development performance analysis on the HRD team, has to prepare the answer. As she starts interviewing program graduates, she talks to Kim Shin-han, a 15-year corporate banking team lead, and Park Hana, a third-year retail banking employee — and realizes the problem isn't a single, simple issue. The two of them took the same program, but for completely different reasons, what they learned never stuck at the company. Lee In-sa has to diagnose why the learning program the organization gave its people isn't translating into organizational performance.
👩‍💼

Lee In-sa

Talent Development Performance Analyst, HRD Team · 32

Lee In-sa has been at GBC for six years. She started out in recruiting and moved to the talent-development performance analysis team two years ago. The role — juggling both hard numbers and people's stories — felt unfamiliar at first, but she's come to feel that digging into the "why" behind the data is exactly the kind of work she's suited for. While putting together this quarter's numbers, she noticed there was no clear correlation between the proportion of program graduates at a branch and that branch's performance metrics. She recalculated it several times, assuming she'd made an error, but got the same result every time. While drafting a report on the finding, she stumbled onto an internal review document from three years earlier — the same issue had essentially been raised back then too, but it had quietly died after the program was given a slightly different name. Realizing how much older this problem is than she'd assumed hit her hard.

"At first I thought I must have miscalculated, so I checked it three times. But the truly strange part came next. I found a document from three years ago, and the same issue had been raised back then, too. And you know what happened? They just changed part of the program's name and called it done. What scares me most is that we're now asking the same question for a second time in five years."
👨‍💼

Kim Shin-han

Team Lead, Corporate Banking (business loans & advisory) · 15 years, Program Cohort 1 graduate · 41

Kim Shin-han has worked with corporate clients ever since he joined the company — for fifteen years now he's been listening to everyone from small manufacturing-company owners to startup founders talk through their finances and business plans, and structuring loans around what he hears. When he was selected for Cohort 1 of the program five years ago, he felt genuinely recognized by the company, and he came away from the leadership coaching and strategic decision-making training feeling like he'd learned a lot. The trouble started once he got back to the branch. When he tried floating a new approach in a team meeting, his branch manager pushed back — "why change something that's already proven to work" — and the other team leads let their skepticism show without saying much out loud. He tried pushing forward on his own a few times, but an effort nobody else backs tends not to last. Lately he's been asking himself a harder question: did he actually want to become a manager, or did he just want to get even better at the work he already does?

"I really do think I learned a lot in that program. But when I got back to the branch and floated a new approach in a meeting, my branch manager said, 'why mess with something that's already proven?' The other team leads didn't say much, but you could read it on their faces. After a while I just didn't have the energy to keep pushing it alone. And honestly, lately I've been thinking — I actually enjoy working out the right loan for each individual client more than I enjoy managing a team. But this program keeps pushing me toward becoming more of a manager."
👩‍💻

Park Hana

Retail Banking (branch-counter service) · 3 years, recent program graduate · 27

Park Hana studied business in college and has worked the customer counter in retail banking at GBC ever since she joined — handling everything from deposit and savings consultations to personal loan guidance, seeing dozens of customers a day. When she was selected for the program in her third year, her peers were openly envious, and she went in with high hopes of her own. But she's had a hard time explaining, even to herself, how material like "global market awareness" and "strategic decision-making" connects to the counter work she actually does every day. Once she learned that completing the program isn't even explicitly factored into promotion reviews, she started feeling less and less sure what those six months actually meant for her career. Lately she's begun to wonder whether this experience — even if it doesn't do much for her inside GBC — might actually be useful if she moved to a foreign bank or a fintech company. She hasn't told anyone this yet, not even her teammates or Lee In-sa.

"What I learned in the program was genuinely interesting. But I work the counter every day, helping customers and processing paperwork, and I honestly don't know when I'd ever use that material. I looked at the promotion review criteria, and completing the program isn't even mentioned. So what were those six months, exactly, for me? ...Actually, I've started thinking something else lately, too. Even if it's no use here, maybe this experience would be worth something somewhere else. I haven't told anyone that yet."
